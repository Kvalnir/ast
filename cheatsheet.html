02 Interaction

Pointing pair

Inside one box, a digit's only homes sit in a single row or column.

Look for
A box with two or three spots for a digit, all sharing a line.
Then delete
That digit, from the rest of the line, outside the box.

Where it goes wrongUsually there is nothing to delete. Check the digit still appears further along the line before you talk yourself into the pattern.

··1111

row 8's 1s are stuck in box 7

03 Interaction

Claiming

Along one line, a digit's only homes sit inside a single box.

Look for
A line with two or three spots for a digit, all inside one box.
Then delete
That digit, from the rest of the box, off the line.

Where it goes wrongEasy to run backwards. The box's 1 is in row 8, so the rest of the row is safe and the rest of the box is not.

04 Subset

Naked pair

Two cells in one unit hold the same two candidates and nothing else.

Look for
Two cells in a unit reading exactly the same two marks.
Then delete
Both digits, from every other cell of that unit.

Where it goes wrongBoth cells must hold nothing but those two digits. A 3/8/9 cell beside a 3/8 cell is not half a pair — it is a target.

139913391339

139 / 13 / 39 across three cells of row 5

05 Subset

Naked triple

Three cells share three candidates between them — no cell needs to hold all three.

Look for
Three cells in a unit whose marks pool to exactly three digits.
Then delete
All three digits, from every other cell of that unit.

Where it goes wrongA cell with four or more marks can never join a triple. Cross those out first and half the unit disappears.

35357···

3 and 5 live only in r2c1 / r2c4, so r2c4 loses its 7

06 Subset

Hidden pair

Two digits in a unit have only two possible cells between them, buried under other candidates.

Look for
Two digits whose footprints in a stuck unit are the same two cells.
Then delete
Every other candidate, from those two cells.

Where it goes wrongOnly pays if one of the cells carries extra marks. Two cells already reading 3/5 is a naked pair with nothing left to delete.

cols 2 and 7, both landing on rows 6 and 7

07 Single digit

X-Wing

One digit, two lines with exactly two spots each, landing in the same two crossing lines.

Look for
Two lines whose two spots share both crossing lines — a rectangle.
Then delete
The digit, from the rest of the two crossing lines.

Where it goes wrongExactly two spots per line. A line with three that happen to include your columns proves nothing, and it is the commonest false positive there is.

9999999999

cols 1, 2, 6 → rows 3, 5, 7

08 Single digit

Swordfish

The X-Wing grown by one: three lines, two or three spots each, confined to three crossing lines.

Look for
Three lines for one digit pooling to exactly three crossings.
Then delete
The digit, from those three crossing lines, outside the three base lines.

Where it goes wrongRarely the real answer at this tier. Re-run the cheap scans first — most apparent swordfish are a pointing pair you walked past.

44444444

rows 3 and 9 share column 4; the ends do the work

09 Single digit

Skyscraper

Two lines with two spots each, sharing one crossing line. One of the two loose ends must be the digit.

Look for
Two rows, two spots each, meeting in one column and parting at the other end.
Then delete
The digit, from any cell that sees both loose ends.

Where it goes wrongTest the loose ends. Sharing the second column too is an X-Wing; sharing a box is a pointing pair. The skyscraper is the near-miss case, where the ends are strangers.

10 Chain

XY-Wing

Three two-candidate cells forming a hinge: XY sees XZ and YZ.

Look for
A bi-value hinge seeing two bi-value cells, each sharing one of its digits plus a common third.
Then delete
That third digit, from any cell seeing both wings.

Where it goes wrongThe hinge must see both wings. The wings do not need to see each other — demanding a neat triangle is how people miss the common case.

A The loop

The stall loop, in six moves

Restart at the top after any successful elimination — one placement usually re-opens the cheap techniques.

  1. Solve every single you can see. Then Autofill Notes, once.
  2. Light each digit in turn: boxes first, for a digit trapped in a line, then lines, for a digit trapped in a box.
  3. Highlight off. Read by density — sparse squares, two marks then three — for pairs and triples.
  4. Light each digit again, counting spots per row and column. Lines scoring exactly two feed X-Wings and skyscrapers.
  5. Use each two-mark square in turn as an XY-Wing hinge.
  6. Apply what you find by hand in Notes mode, and never Autofill again.
B Diagnosis

Read the trigger, not the name

In play these work as triggers rather than as logic: a visual feature appears, and it tells you which tool to reach for. Ordered roughly by how cheap the move is.

  1. One digit's spots in a box sit in one line→ Pointing pair — clear it from the line, outside the box
  2. One digit's spots in a line sit in one box→ Claiming — clear it from the box, off the line
  3. Two cells in a unit show the same two marks→ Naked pair — clear both digits from the rest of the unit
  4. Three sparse cells pooling to three digits→ Naked triple — clear all three from the rest of the unit
  5. Two digits with the same two homes in a unit→ Hidden pair — clear everything else from those two cells
  6. Two lines, two spots each, same crossing pair→ X-Wing — clear the digit from the rest of both crossings
  7. Two lines, two spots each, sharing one crossing only→ Skyscraper — clear it from cells seeing both loose ends
  8. Three lines, two or three spots, three crossings→ Swordfish — clear it from those three crossing lines
  9. A bi-value hinge with two bi-value neighbours→ XY-Wing — clear the shared third digit from cells seeing both wings

If nothing fires, the scan was incomplete or a digit is wrong.
Pick three digits and confirm each still has a legal home in every row, column and box.
A digit with nowhere to go means a mistake several moves back, and no technique will rescue it.

C Vocabulary

Glossary

Unit
Any row, column or box. Nine cells that must hold 1–9 once each.
Candidate
A digit still legal in a cell. Your pencil marks.
Spot
A cell where a given digit is still a candidate. “Two spots” means two possible homes in that unit.
Sees
Two cells see each other if they share a row, column or box. They cannot both hold the same digit.
Strangers
Two cells that see nothing of each other: different row, different column, different box.
Strong link
A unit with exactly two spots for a digit, so one of them must be true.
Loose end
In a skyscraper, the spot on each line that is not in the shared crossing line.
Bi-value cell
A cell with exactly two candidates. Fuel for pairs, wings and chains.
Footprint
The list of spots one digit has in one unit. Read digit-first, not cell-first.
Locked candidates
The umbrella term for pointing pairs and claiming.
Base / crossing line
In fish, the lines you count spots in, and the lines you delete from.
Hinge
In an XY-Wing, the middle cell that sees both wings.
