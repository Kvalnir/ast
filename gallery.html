Advanced Sudoku Techniques · recognition drill

Nine of each. Three are lying.

The reference shows every technique once, on one clean position, and one position is not enough to train an eye. Here is each of the nine on nine boards: six are the real thing and three are not, shuffled, with the answer hidden. Read the figure, decide, then tap it.

The near misses are the point, and none of them is manufactured: each is a real position holding the thing that gets mistaken for the technique — a box with one stray spot, three cells pooling to four digits, two lines that meet twice. Each figure is checked to contain no instance of the technique it is filed under, so a no is a no about the whole picture. If you can tell those three from the six, you can trust yourself at the puzzle.

Amber cells are the pattern you were asked about

Amber lines show how it connects

Ringed cells are the loose ends of a chain

Struck red digits are what it kills

A red ring is the mark that breaks it

Grey is the rest of the position; a dot is a cell with more marks than fit

Single-digit figures show one digit's whole map, the way the News+ highlight does. Subset figures show one unit's marks. XY-Wing figures show every two-mark square on the board. Positions are real, and every elimination claimed is checked against the puzzle's solution before the figure is drawn — see tools/gallery.py.

02 Interaction

Pointing pair

Inside one box, a digit's only homes sit in a single row or column.

Where it goes wrongCount the box first. A third spot for the digit anywhere else in the box, on any other line, and there is nothing here.

03 Interaction

Claiming

Along one line, a digit's only homes sit inside a single box.

Where it goes wrongEasy to run backwards. It is the rest of the box that loses the digit, and only if the line has no spot for it outside that box.

04 Subset

Naked pair

Two cells in one unit hold the same two candidates and nothing else.

Where it goes wrongBoth cells must hold nothing but those two digits. A 3/8/9 beside a 3/8 is not half a pair — it is a target.

05 Subset

Naked triple

Three cells pooling to exactly three candidates — no cell needs all three.

Where it goes wrongA cell with four or more marks can never join a triple, and three cells pooling to four digits is the commonest false positive here.

06 Subset

Hidden pair

Two digits in a unit with only two possible cells between them, buried under other marks.

Where it goes wrongRead the whole unit, not the two cells. A third home anywhere for either digit and the pair is not hidden — it is not there.

07 Single digit

X-Wing

One digit, two lines with exactly two spots each, landing in the same two crossing lines.

Where it goes wrongExactly two spots per line. A line with three that happen to include your columns proves nothing.

08 Single digit

Swordfish

The X-Wing grown by one: three lines, two or three spots each, confined to three crossing lines.

Where it goes wrongThree lines pooling to four crossings is not a swordfish, and that is what most of them turn out to be.

09 Single digit

Skyscraper

Two lines with two spots each, sharing one crossing line. One of the two loose ends must be the digit.

Where it goes wrongTest the loose ends. Sharing the second crossing too is an X-Wing; sharing a box is a pointing pair.

10 Chain

XY-Wing

Three two-candidate cells forming a hinge: XY sees XZ and YZ.

Where it goes wrongThe hinge must see both wings, all three cells must have exactly two marks, and the wings must share a digit the hinge does not hold.

Recognition is a visual skill, so treat this as reps rather than reading: cover the captions, work a row of nine, and only then check. When you want to test a position you are actually looking at rather than one of these, the pattern check takes the cells and marks you can see and audits them condition by condition.
