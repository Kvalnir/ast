11 Uniqueness

Unique rectangle

595915959

rows 2 and 5, columns 8 and 9. 5 from r5c8, 9 from r5c8.

Four cells at the corners of a rectangle, spanning exactly two boxes, all four able to take the same two digits.

Look for
Every time you write a bi-value pair, glance along its row and its column for the same pair again. Two matching pairs on one line is the trigger; then look at the two cells that finish the rectangle, and count the boxes.
Then delete
Type 1 — three corners bare and the fourth carrying extras: the fourth loses both pair digits, which usually solves it outright. Type 2 — all four bare but two of them carrying the same single extra: one of those two is that extra, so everything seeing both loses it.
Because
If all four corners held nothing but A and B, the two ways of filling them would both be legal: swap the diagonals and every row, column and box still reads the same. That is two solutions, and a published puzzle has one. So the position you are looking at cannot be that position — whatever it is that stops the swap has to be true.

Where it goes wrongTwo boxes, not four. Over four boxes the swap would leave a box short of a digit, so there is nothing deadly about it and no argument at all. Four corners reading the same pair across four boxes is the commonest false positive up here, and it looks exactly as convincing as the real thing.

12 Uniqueness

BUG+1

39514·86·71863·54·4265·8·3168241··537994·82561·15···6284571·6·428839254176264781395

r4c9. r4c9 is the 7.

Every unsolved cell is down to two candidates except one, which has three.

Look for
You do not hunt for this one — you notice it. Late in a hard puzzle, when the marks have thinned out to pairs everywhere, look for the single cell still carrying three.
Then delete
A digit placed outright, which is rare enough on this tier to be worth the look.
Because
A grid where every unsolved cell holds two candidates and every digit has exactly two homes in every unit is a bivalue universal grave: its solutions come in pairs, because you can flip every cell to its other candidate and still have a legal grid. One extra candidate is all that stands between the position in front of you and that grave. Take it away and the puzzle would have an even number of answers; it has one; so the extra candidate is the answer in its cell.

Where it goes wrongThree candidates in one cell is not enough on its own. Every other unsolved cell must hold exactly two, and taking the digit out has to leave every digit with exactly two homes in every unit — a unit reading one, three, two has a hidden single in it and is nothing like a grave. The trainer checks the whole board before it offers this, and so should you.

13 Single digit

Finned X-Wing

11111111111111111111111

rows 2 and 8. 1 from r7c5.

An X-Wing with one or two extra spots in a base line, all of them inside a single box.

Look for
This is the pattern you already half-found and threw away. Whenever a line has three spots instead of two, do not abandon the fish — ask whether the third spot sits in the same box as one of the corners. If it does, you have a move.
Then delete
The digit, from the crossing lines — but only inside the fin's box. That is usually one or two cells, and they are the cells the plain X-Wing would have killed anyway.
Because
Either those extra spots are all false — in which case what is left is a plain X-Wing and its eliminations hold — or one of them is the digit. Both branches cost something: a cell that sits in the X-Wing's crossing lines and sees every fin loses the digit either way. Since the fins share a box, seeing all of them means being in that box.

Where it goes wrongAll the fins in one box. Two leftover spots in different boxes and there is no cell that sees both, so nothing survives the second branch. Also check you have the right box: the eliminations are the intersection of the crossing lines with the fin's box, not the whole crossing line.

14 Single digit

2-string kite

5555555555555555555555

row 1 and column 2. 5 from r5c8.

A row and a column with exactly two spots each for one digit, whose near ends share a box.

Look for
Light one digit and write down every line with exactly two spots. Then look for a row and a column from that list with one spot each inside the same box. The far ends are the ones that do the work, and they are usually nowhere near each other.
Then delete
The digit, from every cell that sees both far ends — often just one cell.
Because
Each line is a strong link: one of its two spots is the digit. The two near ends sit in the same box, so they cannot both be it. Kill one and its line's other end becomes the digit; kill the other and the same happens on the other line. Whichever way it falls, one of the two far ends is the digit — so anything seeing both far ends cannot be.

Where it goes wrongIf the near ends share a line rather than a box, this is the skyscraper you already know. If the far ends also line up, it is an X-Wing. And if the near ends share nothing at all, there is no pattern here — two strong links that never meet prove nothing.

15 Single digit

Empty rectangle

5555555555555555555555

box 3, row 2 and column 8. 5 from r5c8.

A box whose spots for one digit all fall on one row and one column of it, joined to a strong link elsewhere.

Look for
Light a digit and look at each box in turn with three or four spots left. Ask whether they fit an L — one row plus one column, leaving a 2×2 block of the box empty. That empty block is what the name is about. Then look along the row and the column for a line with exactly two spots.
Then delete
One cell, almost always. It is a precise move rather than a broad one.
Because
The box has to contain the digit somewhere, and everywhere it could go is on that row or that column: so the box's digit is on one line or the other. Now take a strong link on the same digit with one end on the box's row. If that end is the digit, the box cannot use its row and must use its column; if it is not, the link's far end is the digit. Either way the cell where the far end's line crosses the box's column is finished.

Where it goes wrongThe box's spots must genuinely need both lines. All of them on one line is a pointing pair, which is cheaper and which you should have found already. And the link has to reach the box's row or column with its near end, outside the box itself.

16 Single digit

Simple colouring

2222222222222222222222

4 linked squares on the 2. 2 from r7c1, 2 from r9c1.

Every strong link for one digit, followed at once and coloured in two alternating colours.

Look for
Pick a digit, find any line or box with exactly two spots, and start walking: from each end, look for another two-spot unit through it. Mark alternately as you go — on paper, circles and crosses. Four or five cells in you usually have your answer.
Then delete
Either an entire colour, which can be half a dozen candidates at once, or every cell that sees both colours. This is the widest-reaching move on the tier.
Because
Along a strong link exactly one end is the digit, so a chain of them alternates: true, false, true, false. Colour the alternation and one colour is entirely true and the other entirely false — you just do not know which. Two things follow. If two cells of one colour share a unit, that colour cannot be the true one, so every cell wearing it loses the digit. If a cell outside the chain sees both colours, it loses the digit whichever colour wins.

Where it goes wrongOnly ever chain through strong links — units with exactly two spots. A unit with three is not a link and will colour your grid into nonsense. If following the links round gives one cell both colours, stop: the position is already contradictory, which means an earlier elimination was wrong.

17 Chain

W-Wing

58595913595815959

r2c8 and r5c9, linked on the 5. 9 from r2c9, 9 from r4c8, 9 from r5c8.

Two cells holding the same two candidates, joined by a strong link on one of them.

Look for
Bi-value cells are already on your list. Look for the same pair twice, in cells that do not see each other — then ask whether some unit has exactly two spots for one of the two digits, positioned to reach both.
Then delete
The other digit, from every cell seeing both of the matching pair.
Because
Call the pair A/B, and let the link be on B, with one end seeing the first cell and the other end seeing the second. One of those two ends is a B. Say it is the one next to the first cell: then that cell cannot be B, so it is A. Say it is the other: then the second cell is A. One of the pair is an A either way, so anything seeing both of them cannot be.

Where it goes wrongThe two cells must read exactly the same two digits, and must be strangers to each other. If they see each other you have a naked pair and should have used it already. And the link must be a real strong link on one of the pair's own digits.

18 Chain

XY-chain

582685689484858

4 two-mark squares, r2c2 to r3c8. 8 from r2c7, 8 from r2c8.

A run of two-candidate cells, each seeing the next and sharing a digit with it, that begins and ends on the same digit.

Look for
Start from a bi-value cell and treat one of its digits as the one you are chasing. Step to a neighbour that shares the other digit, and keep stepping. Four links is a normal length; if you have not come back to your starting digit by six, start somewhere else.
Then delete
The chased digit, from every cell seeing both ends of the chain.
Because
Suppose the first cell is not Z. Then it is its other digit; the next cell sees it and shares that digit, so it must be its own other digit; and so on down the chain. Follow it to the end and the last cell is forced to Z. So either the first cell is Z or the last one is — and anything seeing both ends loses Z.

Where it goes wrongEvery cell in the chain must have exactly two candidates — a cell with three breaks the cascade, because being denied one digit no longer forces the other. And the chain has to come back to the digit it started on: ending on a different one proves nothing.

19 Chain

AIC

3581238347834672367

4 links, r2c2 to r8c2. 3 from r3c2.

A chain that alternates strong and weak links and begins and ends on a strong one.

Look for
Not by sweeping — by starting somewhere interesting and walking. Take a candidate that sits in a strong link, follow the link, then leave the cell you land on by a weak link, and repeat. Keep the chain short enough to check: three links is a normal find, five is a long one.
Then delete
Whatever the two ends allow, which is why the ends are the only part worth writing down.
Because
A strong link says at least one of these two is true; a weak link says at most one of these two is true. Alternate them, starting and ending strong, and the chain proves at least one of its two ends is true. That is the whole of it, and it is why the eight techniques above are variations rather than facts: each is an AIC of some particular shape. From the two ends: if both ends are the same digit, everything seeing both loses it; if they are different digits in cells that see each other, neither end can hold the other's digit; if they are two digits in one cell, every other candidate in that cell dies.

Where it goes wrongThe links must genuinely alternate, and the chain must both start and end on a strong link — a chain that ends weak proves nothing whatever it looks like. A strong link is a unit with exactly two spots for the digit, or a cell with exactly two candidates; anything looser is a weak link wearing a disguise.

A The loop

Where these go in the order

Nothing here is worth a minute while a pointing pair is unplayed. Restart at the top after any successful elimination — on this tier one deletion routinely re-opens the cheap techniques.

  1. Run the Advanced loop to its end first. Every time.
  2. Circle the bi-value cells, then look for the same pair twice on a line — unique rectangle.
  3. Go back to the lines with three spots for a digit — finned X-Wing.
  4. From your list of two-spot lines: near ends in a box is a kite, a box filling one row and one column is an empty rectangle.
  5. Colour one digit's whole network. Repeat for the digit with the next most two-spot units.
  6. Matching pairs that are strangers — W-Wing. Then chain the bi-value cells — XY-chain.
  7. Only then build a chain by hand, and keep it to three links if you want to be able to check it.
B Diagnosis

Read the trigger, not the name

What you are looking at, and which of the nine it means. Ordered roughly by what the move costs you to find.

  1. The same two marks on two cells of one line, twice over→ Unique rectangle — count the boxes before you believe it
  2. Every square down to two marks except one→ BUG+1 — the odd square out takes the digit that appears three times
  3. A line with three spots where you wanted two→ Finned X-Wing — if the extra sits in a corner's box, the fish still pays
  4. Two lines of two whose near ends share a box→ 2-string kite — kill what sees both far ends
  5. A box whose spots fill one row and one column→ Empty rectangle — cross it with a two-spot line
  6. One digit with two-spot units all over the grid→ Colour the network — a repeated colour in a unit kills it outright
  7. The same pair of marks in two cells that are strangers→ W-Wing — look for a two-spot unit on one of the digits between them
  8. A run of two-mark cells sharing digits end to end→ XY-chain — it pays if it comes back to the digit it started on
  9. Nothing above fires and the position is tight→ Build an AIC by hand — strong, weak, strong, and read the two ends

If nothing fires, the likeliest explanations are still the cheap ones.
A missed pointing pair, a wrong digit, or an elimination you made by hand that was not sound.
Check those three before you go looking for a longer chain.

C Vocabulary

Glossary

Strong link
A unit with exactly two homes for a digit, or a cell with exactly two candidates: at least one is true.
Weak link
Two candidates that cannot both be true — same digit in one unit, or two digits in one cell.
Alternating chain
Strong, weak, strong … starting and ending strong. It proves at least one end is true.
Fin
A spot in a fish's base line outside the covering lines. Harmless if one box sees all of it.
Near end / far end
In a kite, the two ends sharing a box, and the two that do not. The far ones do the work.
Colour
One side of an alternating network. All true or all false, and you do not know which.
Deadly pattern
Four cells on a rectangle in two boxes holding the same two candidates. A unique puzzle cannot contain one.
Grave (BUG)
A position where every unsolved cell holds two candidates and every digit has two homes per unit. Its answers come in pairs.
Degrades safely
What a technique does on a grid you have broken: finds nothing, rather than finding something wrong. Every technique here does except the two that reason from uniqueness.
D Honesty

Two of these trust the puzzle, not your grid

The unique rectangle and BUG+1 argue from the puzzle having exactly one answer. Every other technique on both tiers reads only what is in front of it, and finds nothing on a grid you have corrupted; these two keep working and hand you a confident wrong elimination instead. The trainer will not offer either unless the digits on your board still lead to a single solution — the long version is here.
