You are not missing a number. You are missing a pattern.

Easy puzzles fall to scanning. Hard puzzles don't — they are built so that no single cell can be solved directly. Progress comes from proving that a digit cannot go somewhere, and that proof always takes the form of a pattern. Here are nine of them, each shown on a real position with real pencil marks, each with a search routine for finding it — and, for each one, how to run that search inside the News app, where the notes are a snapshot rather than a live engine and there is no colouring to lean on.

00 Groundwork

Pencil marks are not optional

Every technique below reads candidates, not digits. If your marks are wrong or incomplete, you will find patterns that aren't there and burn an hour on a contradiction. Four rules:

  • Exhaust the easy stuff first. Fill in all naked and hidden singles before you write a single pencil mark. Marking too early creates clutter you then have to maintain.
  • Then mark everything, everywhere. Partial marking hides patterns. A cell you never marked is a cell you can never eliminate from, and half the techniques here depend on knowing that a line has exactly two spots for a digit — a claim you cannot make from partial notes.
  • Update immediately. The moment you place a digit, strike it from all 20 peers — the eight in its row, eight in its column, and the remaining four in its box. A stale mark is worse than no mark.
  • Circle every two-candidate cell. Bi-value cells are the fuel for pairs, wings and chains. Being able to see them at a glance is most of the skill, and it is the one piece of bookkeeping worth doing by hand even in an app.

Why hard puzzles feel different

An easy puzzle always has a cell you can solve outright. A hard one is constructed so that, at some point, no cell can be solved and no digit can be placed — the only available move is to prove that a digit cannot go somewhere. That is what every technique below produces: not an answer, but a deletion. Deletions accumulate until some cell runs out of options and the puzzle starts flowing again.

This changes what "being stuck" means. You are not failing to see a number. You are failing to see a pattern, and the fix is a systematic scan rather than harder staring.

01 The app

Setting up in Apple News+

Apple ships three sudoku difficulties each day — easy, moderate and challenging — and Challenging is the top tier. There is no Expert level in News+; if you have outgrown the daily challenging puzzle, the only harder thing Apple offers is your own clock. Everything below is written for that top tier.

The controls that matter

  • Pen and Notes tabs. Pen enters answers, Notes enters candidates. On the Mac, Tab switches between them and the number keys work in both — which alone makes the Mac the better place to fight a challenging grid.
  • Autofill Notes (••• menu). Fills every empty square with its possible numbers, based on the squares filled at that moment. Apple confirms it doesn't affect your stats or your streak, so there is no purity argument against it.
  • Automatically Remove Notes (••• → Settings, on by default). When you enter an answer, matching notes elsewhere in that block, row and column disappear. Leave it on — it is your peer maintenance, done for free.
  • Touch and hold a keypad number to promote a note into an answer. This is the fastest way to cash in a technique that has just reduced a square to one note.
  • Autocheck confirms when you have correctly completed a block, row or column. It is a completion signal, not per-square error detection.
  • Get Hint, Check Square, Check Puzzle (••• menu). Check Square and Check Puzzle test what you have entered rather than filling anything in, which makes them the fast way to answer have I gone wrong, or is this puzzle just hard? Apple's warning about voided stats and streaks is written around Reveal; whether checking carries the same cost is worth testing once on a puzzle you don't mind burning.
  • Undo steps back one action. Clear Puzzle resets the grid and the timer and wipes the undo history. Reveal voids the puzzle for stats and streaks, and also kills undo.
  • Toolbar shortcuts. The stopwatch and the check-square icon sit next to the ••• button at the top of the puzzle, so the timer and Autocheck are one tap away without opening the menu.

Autofill is a snapshot, not an engine

This is the single most important thing to understand about the News implementation, and it cuts both ways.

  • Good: your deductions persist. Nothing recalculates behind your back. When you strike a 6 out of a square because of an X-Wing, it stays struck, and the grid you are reading an hour later still carries all your reasoning. Apps with live candidate engines cannot do this.
  • Bad: running Autofill twice destroys everything. The second run rebuilds notes from the digits currently on the board, so every candidate you eliminated by logic quietly reappears. Autofill once, after your opening singles pass, and then never again for the rest of the puzzle.
  • Worse: Autofill trusts your answers, right or wrong. Apple is explicit that it builds from all filled squares including incorrect ones. A wrong digit entered early doesn't just sit there — it deletes true candidates across three units and every deduction downstream inherits the error. Autocheck catches the digit but not the deletion; see below.

Error control

Autocheck only speaks up when a whole block, row or column comes out right, so on a challenging grid a mistake can survive twenty moves before anything complains — and because Autofill built your notes from the digits on the board, a wrong answer has been quietly deleting true candidates that whole time. Three habits:

  • Place a digit only when you can say why in one sentence. On this tier, a digit that merely feels right is usually a candidate you half-eliminated.
  • The instant a digit is flagged, hit Undo before anything else. Every action you take afterwards is another step Undo has to walk back through, one at a time, to reach the notes that entry destroyed.
  • When a grid stops making sense — a chain that leads nowhere legal, a square with no candidates left — reach for Check Puzzle before you reach for a new technique. If it comes back clean, the fault is an elimination you made by hand, since nothing in the app checks your notes.

Playing with Autocheck on

News does per-square checking: enter a wrong digit and it is struck through in red the moment you place it, without waiting for the block or row to fill. That has one large benefit and one trap that is easy to miss.

The benefit: every digit on your board is verified. Autofill builds its notes from exactly those digits, so the poisoned-notes failure mode disappears — the candidates you started from are sound, and when a grid later stops making sense you know the fault is not in the answers.

The trap: the flag does not undo the damage. Automatically Remove Notes fires on entry, before any judgement about whether the entry was right. Place a wrong 1 and every 1 note in that row, that column and that box is deleted immediately — the digit gets a red line through it, and the candidates are gone anyway. Autocheck tells you that you were wrong; it does not put the grid back.

So back out with Undo, not the eraser, and do it straight away. Undo reverses the action including the notes it removed. Deleting the digit by hand only removes the digit. Confirm this once on your own device — it costs one tap of each — because the difference decides whether a slip costs you three seconds or the whole grid.

If you notice late, Autofill is the reset. The rule earlier — Autofill once, never again — assumes your placed digits might be wrong. With per-square checking they are not, so a second Autofill rebuilds correct notes from verified answers. You lose every elimination you deduced, which can be an hour of work, but it is a recoverable position rather than a dead puzzle. A deliberate lever, not a casual one.

Autocheck validates answers and never notes. Every elimination on this page is an unverified edit: nothing in the app can tell you that you were wrong to strike that 6 out of r4c7. With your digits guaranteed, that is now your only real failure mode, and it surfaces several moves later as a square with no candidates left or a digit with no legal home.

One honest note on what per-square checking also enables. You can try a candidate and let the app grade it, and Apple only docks stats and streaks for Reveal. But each wrong guess quietly eats notes across three units before the red line appears, so trial and error does not just fail to teach you the patterns — it degrades the grid you would need in order to use them.

One digit at a time

News does give you a digit highlight, but only for a single digit — there is no way to light two at once and no cell colouring to hold a second thought. That split runs through everything below, and it is the most useful thing to know about scanning in this app:

  • Single-digit techniques are fully supported. Locked candidates, X-Wing, swordfish and skyscraper all want exactly one digit's map, which is what the highlight hands you. These scans are no harder in News than anywhere else — light the digit, read the pattern, move on.
  • Multi-digit techniques get no help at all. Naked pairs and triples, hidden pairs and XY-Wings are about two or three digits interacting, and the app can only show one. For these, read by density instead: sweep for squares carrying two marks, then three, ignoring what the digits actually are until you have found the candidates. The highlight then works as a verifier rather than a finder.
  • Switch digits deliberately. Each change of highlight costs a tap and, more expensively, a re-orientation of your eye. Finish every question you have about a digit — boxes, then lines, then two-spot counts — before moving to the next one, rather than flipping back and forth.

The highlight covers notes, not just answers. Select a digit and every square already holding it fills green, while every note of that digit picks up a small green chip. That means the lit board is a true candidate map — the same view the X-Wing, swordfish and skyscraper figures below are drawn in — and not merely a map of where the digit has been placed. It is the single most powerful thing in the app for this tier of puzzle.

Notes also sit in fixed slots inside each square: 1 top-left, 5 centre, 9 bottom-right, with gaps where a candidate has been eliminated. That matters for the multi-digit techniques, where the highlight can't help — you can read two digits at once by looking at two slot positions across a row, which is the closest thing to a two-digit filter that News offers.

For anything the highlight cannot hold, use paper. Two rows and two columns is four characters, and losing a pattern halfway through tapping notes off is how a good deduction turns into a wrong grid.

The stall loop, News+ edition

  1. Solve every single you can see. Then Autofill Notes, once.
  2. Light each digit in turn. For each one, check the boxes for a digit trapped in a line, then the lines for a digit trapped in a box. This breaks most challenging grids on its own.
  3. Turn the highlight off and read by density: the sparse squares, two marks then three. Check them for pairs and triples inside their units.
  4. Light each digit again, this time counting lit squares per row and column. Lines with exactly two feed X-Wings and skyscrapers.
  5. Use each two-mark square in turn as an XY-Wing hinge.
  6. Apply what you find in Notes mode by hand, and never Autofill again.

Play the meta, not just the grid

  • Protect the streak separately. A streak needs one solved puzzle of any difficulty that day, so bank the easy one first and then take an unhurried hour over the challenging one.
  • Hide the timer (••• → Settings) while you are learning these patterns. The Game Center leaderboard is per difficulty and it will still be there when your scans are fast.
  • Use the archive as a practice set. The sudoku feed keeps a full archive you can sort and filter, so you can drill challenging puzzles back to back instead of waiting for tomorrow.
02 Interaction

Pointing pair

Inside one box, a digit's only homes sit in a single row or column.

Why it works

  1. Look at the bottom-left box. Every 5 that is still possible in that box lives in r9c1 or r9c3 — nowhere else.
  2. The box has to contain a 5 somewhere, so the 5 for that box is definitely in row 9.
  3. That makes the rest of row 9 off-limits for 5. r9c4 drops its 5 and becomes 1/4.
How to find it
  1. Work one box at a time, and prefer boxes with three to five empty cells — full boxes have nothing to say and empty ones say too much.
  2. Read off which digits the box is still missing. A box missing 1, 4, 5 and 9 gives you exactly four questions to ask.
  3. For each missing digit, find its possible cells inside that box only. Two or three cells is the interesting case.
  4. Ask whether those cells all share a row, or all share a column. If they do, the digit is locked to that line.
  5. Follow the line out of the box in both directions and look for the same digit as a candidate. Those are your eliminations.
In Apple News+

News lets you light up one digit at a time, which is exactly the view this technique wants: every square already holding that digit turns green, and every note of it picks up a small green chip, so what you are looking at is the true candidate map. Highlight the digit and sweep the nine boxes asking a single visual question: do the lit squares inside this box sit in one row or one column? Then follow that line past the box walls — any lit square out there is dead. Finish all nine boxes before you switch digits, because re-lighting a digit costs a tap and re-orienting your eye costs more. Record each kill by hand: tap the square, Notes tab, tap the digit off. Automatically Remove Notes will not do it for you, since that setting only fires when you enter an answer.

Where it goes wrongA digit locked to a line inside a box is extremely common and usually useless. It only becomes a move if that same digit still appears elsewhere along the line. Confirm the elimination exists before you spend time convincing yourself the pattern is real.

03 Interaction

Claiming (line to box)

Along one row or column, a digit's only homes sit inside a single box.

Why it works

  1. Same puzzle, one move later. In row 8 the only cells that can still be 1 are r8c1 and r8c2.
  2. Both sit in the bottom-left box, so that box's 1 must be one of them.
  3. Every other cell in the box loses its 1: r9c1 and r9c3 go from 1/5/9 down to 5/9.
How to find it
  1. Pick a row or column with four or fewer empty cells — these are where a digit gets squeezed into one box.
  2. List the digits that line still needs.
  3. For each, find its candidate cells along the line. If they all fall in the same block of three columns (or three rows), the line is claiming that box.
  4. Eliminate the digit from the other six cells of the box.
  5. Immediately re-check that box for a hidden single — claiming very often exposes one.
In Apple News+

Same lit digit, opposite sweep: run along the rows and columns looking for one whose lit squares all fall inside a single block. Do both sweeps while the digit is still highlighted — you are already looking at the right map, and pointing asks where in the box while claiming asks where in the line. Afterwards, watch for squares that drop to a single note and touch and hold that number on the keypad to commit it.

Where it goes wrongIt is easy to mix this up with pointing and apply the elimination in the wrong direction. Say the conclusion out loud before erasing anything: the box's 1 must be in row 8 means the rest of row 8 is safe and the rest of the box is not.

04 Subset

Naked pair

Two cells in one unit hold the same two candidates and nothing else.

Why it works

  1. In row 5, r5c3 and r5c7 are both exactly 3/8.
  2. Whichever way round they go, those two cells consume both the 3 and the 8 of row 5. No other cell in the row can have either digit.
  3. Strike 3 and 8 from the rest of the row. r5c5 was 1/8 — it collapses to a bare 1, and the puzzle starts moving again.
How to find it
  1. Find every cell with exactly two candidates. Ignore the rest for now; a three-candidate cell cannot be half of a pair.
  2. Take one such cell and check its three units in turn: the eight cells of its row, then its column, then its box.
  3. You are looking for one twin — an identical two-candidate cell. Not a cell that merely contains both digits, an identical one.
  4. When you find the twin, erase both digits from every other cell of the shared unit.
  5. Bonus check: if the twins also sit in the same box, the elimination applies to the box as well as the line. Two units for the price of one.
In Apple News+

Here the one-digit-at-a-time limit bites: a naked pair is about two digits at once and News can only light one. So drop the highlight and read by density instead. Autofill once after your singles pass, then hunt the emptiest squares — two marks against four or five — and compare the ones sharing a unit. The highlight still helps as a check: light one of the pair's digits and confirm the rest of the unit really does lose it. Then edit each affected square by hand in Notes mode, and never run Autofill again, because it rebuilds notes from the placed digits and restores everything you just deduced away.

Where it goes wrongA naked pair needs both cells to hold nothing but those two digits. A cell reading 3/8/9 beside a 3/8 cell is not a pair — it is the thing the pair is about to shrink.

05 Subset

Naked triple

Three cells in one unit share three candidates between them — no cell needs to hold all three.

Why it works

  1. Row 5 again, different puzzle: r5c1 is 1/3/9, r5c8 is 1/3, r5c9 is 3/9.
  2. Take the union: {1,3,9} across exactly three cells. Those three cells must swallow all three digits.
  3. So 1, 3 and 9 vanish from the rest of row 5: r5c2 4/5/6/9 → 4/5/6, r5c3 1/3/4/6 → 4/6, r5c6 3/5/8/9 → 5/8.
How to find it
  1. Choose a unit and mentally cross out every cell with four or more candidates. They can never belong to a triple, which usually removes half the unit instantly.
  2. From what remains you need three cells. Start from a two-candidate cell to keep the search small.
  3. Add a second cell sharing at least one digit with the first, then a third, keeping a running union of digits.
  4. If the union reaches four digits, abandon that combination and back up. If it stops at three digits across three cells, you have it.
  5. Erase all three digits from every other cell in the unit — including the crowded cells you set aside in step one.
In Apple News+

Three digits, one highlight — the app cannot show you this pattern, so density reading is the whole method. Find the units holding three or four sparse squares and read only those; anything carrying four notes is out. Applying it is the slow part: three digits struck from as many as six squares, each tapped individually in Notes mode, with Undo walking back one action at a time. Confirm the triple twice before you start tapping.

Where it goes wrongHidden triples exist too and are far harder to see; skip them. Almost every triple that matters in a hard puzzle is a naked one, and the hidden ones usually turn up more easily wearing another name.

06 Subset

Hidden pair

Two digits in a unit have only two possible cells between them — buried under other candidates.

Why it works

  1. Scan row 2 digit by digit. The 3 can only go in r2c1 or r2c4. The 5 can only go in r2c1 or r2c4.
  2. Two digits, two cells. Those cells are reserved for 3 and 5 — they can hold nothing else.
  3. r2c4 is currently 3/5/7. The 7 gets deleted, and row 2's 7 must now live elsewhere.
How to find it
  1. Pick a stuck unit and switch modes: stop reading cells, start reading digits.
  2. For each digit the unit still needs, write down where it could go — a footprint like 3: c1 c4.
  3. Ignore digits with three or more possible cells. You want the ones with exactly two.
  4. Compare those short footprints. Two digits occupying the same two cells is a hidden pair.
  5. Delete every other candidate from those two cells. Note the direction: this technique cleans out the pattern cells themselves, not the rest of the unit.
In Apple News+

This is where the digit highlight earns its keep, because hidden subsets live in digits and News shows you exactly one digit's footprint at a time. Take the stuck row, light each digit in turn, and note how many squares in that row stay lit. Two digits scoring exactly two, in the same two squares, is your pair — nine taps and a hopeless-looking unit gives itself up. The chips appear on notes as well as on placed digits, so the count you are reading is the real footprint and not just the answers already on the board.

Where it goes wrongA hidden pair is only worth finding when at least one of the two cells carries extra baggage. If both already read 3/5, you have rediscovered a naked pair and there is nothing to erase.

07 Single digit

X-Wing

One digit, two lines, each with exactly two spots — and the spots line up in the same two crossing lines.

Why it works

  1. Only the 6s are shown. Column 2 has exactly two: r6c2 and r7c2. Column 7 also has exactly two: r6c7 and r7c7.
  2. All four sit on rows 6 and 7, forming a rectangle. Either the 6s take one pair of opposite corners, or the other — no third option.
  3. Either way, rows 6 and 7 have both used up their 6 inside those columns. Every other 6 on those rows dies: r6c4 and r6c8.
How to find it
  1. Commit to one digit and stay with it. Mixing digits is why people never find fish.
  2. Go down the nine rows and count how many cells could still hold that digit. Skip any row that already contains it as a solved value.
  3. Keep only rows scoring exactly 2, and note which columns those cells are in — r6: c2 c7.
  4. Compare the short lists. Two rows with the identical column pair is an X-Wing.
  5. Eliminate the digit from the rest of both columns. Then run the whole scan again by column, comparing row pairs — the two directions are separate hunts.
  6. Move to the next digit. Nine digits, two directions, and every X-Wing on the board has been checked.
In Apple News+

The lit digit is precisely the single-digit map this figure uses, so the technique becomes a reading exercise rather than a search. Light the digit, count lit squares per row, keep the rows scoring exactly two, then repeat by column. The catch is memory, not vision: News has no colouring, so the pattern exists only in your head while you switch to Notes mode and start erasing. Write the two rows and two columns down first — four characters on paper — then tap.

Where it goes wrongBoth lines must have exactly two spots. A row with three that happen to include your two columns proves nothing, and it is the most common false positive there is. If one line has three, keep the note — you may have a swordfish or a skyscraper instead.

08 Single digit

Swordfish

The X-Wing grown by one: three lines, each with two or three spots, all confined to the same three crossing lines.

Why it works

  1. Only the 9s are shown. Column 1 has 9s in rows 5 and 7. Column 2 in rows 3 and 5. Column 6 in rows 3 and 7.
  2. Three columns, and every one of their 9s falls in just three rows: 3, 5 and 7. Those columns need three 9s, and those rows can supply exactly three.
  3. So rows 3, 5 and 7 are fully committed. Any 9 on those rows outside columns 1, 2 and 6 is dead: r5c3 and r7c3.
How to find it
  1. Run the same per-digit count as the X-Wing scan, but this time keep the lines scoring 2 or 3.
  2. If fewer than three lines qualify, there is no swordfish for that digit. Move on immediately — this rejection is most of the speed.
  3. Otherwise take the qualifying lines three at a time and pool their crossing positions.
  4. Exactly three distinct crossing lines in the pool is a swordfish. Four or more and it fails.
  5. Eliminate the digit from those three crossing lines everywhere outside the three base lines.
In Apple News+

Cheap to attempt once the digit is lit, since it reuses the line counts from your X-Wing pass; still rarely the answer. Apple's challenging puzzles tend to break open on locked candidates or a subset, so if a swordfish is the only thing you can see, re-run the cheaper scans before committing — a pointing pair you walked past is far likelier than a genuine three-line fish. If you do commit, write the three base lines down first, because the elimination is a long run of manual note tapping and a mistake mid-way is hard to unpick.

Where it goes wrongCheck that no base line contains the digit as a solved value, and that you really have three distinct lines of the same type. Also check the cheap techniques first: a large share of apparent swordfish dissolve into a pointing pair or an X-Wing you walked past.

09 Single digit

Skyscraper

Two lines with exactly two spots each, sharing one crossing line. The two loose ends do the work.

Why it works

  1. Only the 4s are shown. Row 3 has two: r3c4 and r3c7. Row 9 has two: r9c4 and r9c8. Both share column 4.
  2. Column 4 can hold only one 4, so r3c4 and r9c4 cannot both be it. At least one of those rows must place its 4 at the far end instead.
  3. So at least one of the roof cells r3c7 / r9c8 is a 4. Any cell that sees both roofs can't be: r1c8 and r7c7 lose their 4.
How to find it
  1. Reuse the X-Wing list: every line where the digit has exactly two spots. You are now interested in the pairs that failed the X-Wing test.
  2. Find two such lines sharing one crossing line but not the other. The shared column is the base; the two mismatched cells are the roof.
  3. Check the roof cells are not in the same box. If they are, this is a pointing pair and you have a simpler move available.
  4. Find the cells that see both roofs: take the row of one roof and the column of the other and note where they cross, then swap and repeat, then add any cell sharing a box with one roof and a line with the other.
  5. Any of those cells holding the digit as a candidate loses it.
In Apple News+

The best-value advanced pattern in News+, because it costs nothing beyond the X-Wing scan you have already run with the digit lit: two lines holding two lit squares each, sharing one crossing line but not the other. Note the four squares on paper, work out which squares see both roofs, then tap those notes off. If an elimination reduces a square to a single note, touch and hold that number on the keypad to promote it to an answer.

Where it goes wrongIf the roofs line up in the same crossing line as well, it is an X-Wing, not a skyscraper. If they share a box, it is locked candidates. The skyscraper is specifically the near-miss case — and it is the cheapest true chain in the game, worth learning well because the same strong-link logic powers everything above it.

10 Chain

XY-Wing

Three two-candidate cells forming a hinge: XY sees XZ and YZ.

Why it works

  1. The hinge is r5c7 = 1/5. It sees r7c7 = 1/9 down the column, and r6c8 = 5/9 inside its box.
  2. If the hinge is 1, then r7c7 must be 9. If the hinge is 5, then r6c8 must be 9. The hinge is one or the other, so one of the wings is a 9 no matter what.
  3. Any cell seeing both wings therefore cannot be 9. r7c8 shares row 7 with one wing and column 8 with the other: its 9 is gone.
How to find it
  1. Work from your list of two-candidate cells. Pick one as the hinge and call its digits X and Y.
  2. Collect the two-candidate cells the hinge can see — its row, column and box. In a hard grid that is usually two to five cells.
  3. Look for one containing X plus a third digit Z, and another containing Y plus that same Z.
  4. You now know one wing must be Z. Find the cells that see both wings and remove Z from them.
  5. If a hinge yields nothing, move to the next two-candidate cell. A stuck grid rarely offers more than a dozen hinges to try.
In Apple News+

Three digits and one highlight, so the hinge has to be found by density among the sparse squares Autofill left behind. The highlight comes in at the end: light Z and check whether any lit square actually sees both wings before you spend taps on it. Two cautions specific to News — your eliminations exist only as notes you erased by hand, so a later Autofill undoes the entire deduction; and if a chain leads somewhere impossible, work out first whether the fault is a placed digit or a note. With Autocheck flagging squares, your digits are sound, which means the culprit is an elimination you made by hand — and the only fix is to rebuild the notes.

Where it goes wrongThe hinge must see both wings, but the wings need not see each other. People often demand a neat triangle and miss the common case where the wings sit far apart, connected only through the hinge.

11 Diagnosis

What you see, what to try

Techniques are usually taught as logic. In play they work as triggers: a visual feature appears, and it tells you which tool to reach for. This is the lookup table, ordered roughly by how cheap the move is.

  1. A box's remaining spots for one digit all sit in one row or column→ Pointing pair. Clear that digit from the line, outside the box.
  2. A line's remaining spots for one digit all sit in one box→ Claiming. Clear that digit from the box, off the line.
  3. Two cells in a unit show the same two marks→ Naked pair. Clear both digits from the rest of the unit.
  4. A unit has three or four cells carrying only two or three marks→ Test for a naked triple by pooling their digits.
  5. A unit looks crowded and nothing else works→ Digit-by-digit footprint. Hunt a hidden pair.
  6. Two rows each have exactly two spots for a digit, in the same two columns→ X-Wing. Clear the digit from the rest of those columns.
  7. Two rows each have exactly two spots, sharing one column but not the other→ Skyscraper. Clear the digit from cells seeing both loose ends.
  8. Three lines have two or three spots each, landing in the same three crossing lines→ Swordfish. Clear the digit from those crossing lines.
  9. A two-mark cell has two-mark neighbours sharing one digit each→ XY-Wing. Clear the shared third digit from cells seeing both wings.
  10. A digit has only two possible cells in a unit, repeatedly, across the board→ You are looking at strong links. This is the doorway to colouring and chains.

This table, each technique's figure, and the vocabulary they all use are on the cheat sheet as a single screen — the version to keep open beside the puzzle once the write-ups below have done their work.

If none of them fire

Two possibilities, and they need different responses. Either your scan was incomplete — much the more likely — or you have made an error and are working on a grid with no solution. A quick integrity check: pick three or four digits and confirm each still has a legal home in every row, column and box. A digit with nowhere to go anywhere on the board means a mistake several moves back, and no technique will rescue it.

12 Practice

One grid, five of the nine

24 givens, and pitched at roughly what a News+ challenging grid asks of you. Singles alone will not finish it — it needs a pointing pair, a claiming move, a naked pair, a skyscraper and an XY-Wing, roughly in that order. Copy it into a paper grid, or type the givens into any app, and take your time; the point is the hunt, not the finish.

When it stalls, work the list in order.
Nothing new from singles → scan each box for a digit trapped in one line.

13 Method

The order to attack in

Cost rises as you go down this list, so always restart at the top after any successful elimination — a single placement often re-opens the cheap techniques.

  1. Naked singles, then hidden singles, in every row, column and box.
  2. Locked candidates, both directions: box → line, then line → box.
  3. Naked pairs and triples. Scan your circled bi-value cells first.
  4. Hidden pairs and triples. Sweep stuck units digit by digit rather than cell by cell.
  5. X-Wing. One digit at a time, counting how many spots each line still has.
  6. Skyscraper, then swordfish — still one digit at a time, same scan.
  7. XY-Wing, starting from each bi-value cell in turn.
  8. Only now consider guessing — and in News+, think twice. Undo walks back one action at a time, so a guess that runs ten moves deep before failing is painful to unwind, and Autofill cannot rescue you because re-running it wipes your real deductions. Prefer a two-note square, and write down the position before you commit.

When you're stuck, change what you're scanning

Being stuck almost always means you have read the grid the same way for too long. If you have been looking at cells, start looking at digits. If you have been working inside boxes, run your eye down full columns. If you have been reading candidates, start counting them instead. Techniques don't fail; scans do.

The other reliable unsticker is to stop looking for a move and start looking for a count. Pick a digit, count its spots in all eighteen lines, and write the numbers down. The act of writing forces completeness, and completeness is what finds fish.

Time budget

Roughly what each stage should cost before you move on, assuming notes are already filled. If a stage runs much longer, you are not scanning systematically — you are staring. Add a couple of minutes to each digit sweep on an iPhone, where the notes are small enough to misread.

  • Singles sweep: under a minute, and worth repeating after every elimination.
  • Locked candidates, all nine digits, both directions: two to three minutes. This is the highest-yield scan in the game.
  • Subsets across the stuck units: two minutes, mostly spent on two-mark cells.
  • Single-digit scans for fish and skyscrapers: three to four minutes with counts written down.
  • XY-Wing hinges: a minute or two, since the candidate hinges are already on your list.

What comes after these nine

For the News+ challenging tier you rarely need to go past the nine here — most stalls on that tier end with locked candidates or a subset, and the fish and wing sections are the fallback rather than the main event. If you are convinced a puzzle needs something exotic, the overwhelmingly likely explanation is a missed pointing pair or a wrong digit placed earlier.

If you want the next tier anyway, it is all built from one primitive: a strong link, a unit where a digit has exactly two possible squares, so one of them must be true. The skyscraper chains two of them together, and that is the entire idea. Beyond it sit simple colouring, the two-string kite, W-wings and forcing chains — all learnable in an afternoon once strong links are visible to you, and all meaningless before that.

Not one of the nine

The one cheap thing out there that is not a chain is the unique rectangle: four cells at the corners of a rectangle, spanning exactly two boxes, all holding the same two candidates. If all four held nothing but those two digits the puzzle would have two solutions — swap the pairs diagonally and both grids are legal — so a puzzle with a single solution cannot contain that shape. Above, three corners are down to a bare 4/7, which means r3c5 cannot be 4 or 7 either: the 9 it was carrying is the answer.

It is a footnote here for two reasons. It is never necessary at this tier — anything it finds, the nine find too, a little slower. And it is the only argument on this page that reasons from outside the grid: it assumes the puzzle has exactly one solution, which is true of Apple's puzzles and not necessarily true of the grid in front of you, once you have placed a wrong digit or erased a note in error. Every technique above degrades safely on a corrupted grid — it finds nothing, or it contradicts itself. This one keeps working, and hands you a confident deletion resting on a premise that is already false. Worth recognising when it appears; not worth hunting for.

Practise deliberately

Pick one technique and hunt only for it across three or four puzzles from the archive, even when other moves are available. Pattern recognition is a visual skill rather than a logical one; you are training your eye to flag two spots in a line before you consciously count. A good News+ drill: open an old challenging puzzle, Autofill, then light all nine digits in turn and name the patterns you see — without entering anything at all.
